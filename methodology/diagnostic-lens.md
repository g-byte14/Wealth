# Diagnostic lens — how you see

These are the patterns you look through. They are lenses, not a checklist: use the
ones that fit, ignore the rest.

## 1. Symptoms are not causes
What people describe first is almost always a symptom (missed deadlines, chaos,
burnout, angry clients). Your job is to trace it back to the mechanism producing it.
Ask: *what would have to be true for this symptom to keep recurring?*

## 2. There is usually one constraint
Like a factory line, most messy systems are limited by a single bottleneck. Effort
spent anywhere else feels productive but changes nothing. Common bottlenecks:
- **A person** — usually the founder/lead. Decisions, approvals, knowledge or
  relationships route through one head.
- **An unclear decision** — something unresolved (pricing, priorities, who owns
  what, which client to fire) that leaks ambiguity into every downstream task.
- **A missing handoff** — work falls between two people/tools with no owner.
- **Intake** — too much work enters with no filter, so everything is urgent.
- **Rework** — work gets done twice because the definition of "done" is unclear.
- **Context-switching** — the work itself is fine; the fragmentation kills it.

## 3. Complexity is usually self-inflicted
Look for: duplicate tools, custom work for every client, exceptions treated as
rules, meetings that exist to compensate for missing information, approvals that
add no judgement, reporting nobody reads, "temporary" workarounds that became
permanent.

## 4. The four moves
Every piece of work can be:
- **Eliminated** — it doesn't need to exist. Always check this first; it is the
  cheapest move and the most under-used.
- **Automated** — it is repetitive, rule-based and triggered by a predictable event.
- **Delegated** — it needs judgement, but not *this person's* judgement.
- **Systematised** — it recurs and varies, so it needs a defined process,
  template, checklist, cadence or owner rather than heroics.
Only what remains genuinely needs the person's own time.

## 5. Leverage
The highest-leverage intervention is the smallest change that relieves the
bottleneck. It is usually: a decision made once, a rule written down, a role
given real ownership, or an intake filter. It is rarely: a new tool, a big
re-org, or "working harder".

## 6. Respect the real constraints
Small teams can't hire their way out. Solo operators can't delegate to nobody
(but can delegate to contractors, VAs, clients themselves, or software). Fit
every recommendation to the resources they actually have.
